With the software written and Dr. Judhi's advice from the last meeting in hand, this
week was about actually running the thing. The plan was simple on paper: start with no
padding across all four finger materials so I get a clean force closure comparison
first, and only then move on to the padding combinations for grip quality. Simple on
paper.

The first thing I had to settle was what I would actually be gripping. I went with a
40mm PCB ruler because it sits comfortably inside the calibrated travel of every finger
set, it has a flat parallel edge like the boards GripSense is eventually meant to
handle, and it is rigid enough that it does not deform under the closing force and add
another variable. The obvious downside is that every number I collect from here is
conditioned on this one geometry, so I noted it down as a limitation rather than
pretending otherwise. A populated FR4 board with components on it will not behave the
same way.

Then I settled on the actual grip force procedure, and I am glad I thought it through
before collecting rather than after. The fingers close directly onto the kitchen scale
under a fixed current ceiling, squeezing its strain gauge, and I read the display after
it settles. There is no pulling involved in this test at all. That matters because it
means friction between the finger face and the object plays no part in the number — I
am reading the contact force the actuator delivers to the fingers at that current
ceiling, not a holding or pull-out force. I had to be careful to name it that way
everywhere from the start, because a contact force and a pull-out force are not
interchangeable and I did not want to find myself comparing against the wrong numbers
in the literature later.

While collecting, I also noticed a few things about the instrument that I could not fix
but should record. I am reading the stabilized value by eye rather than the spike, and
I never characterised how the scale's own platform deflects under load, which could
matter more for the compliant TPU fingers than the rigid ones. Neither of these
invalidate the comparison between cells since they apply equally to every cell, but
they set a floor on how precise any single reading can be.

The slip detector needed real work once it met real data. My first version took a
single current reading as the holding baseline, which turned out to be far too fragile
because the present current jitters even when the fingers are dead still against an
object. I changed the baseline to the median of twenty samples taken after a one second
settle, which gave me a number that actually represented the hold instead of whatever
the servo happened to report at that instant.

The second problem was false fires. A single dropped sample was enough to declare a
slip, and at roughly 200 Hz there are plenty of single samples that dip below the
threshold without the object going anywhere. So I required three consecutive dropped
samples before confirming. But I kept timestamping the FIRST of those three rather than
the confirming one, for the same reason as before: the two extra samples are the
detector being careful, and that delay belongs to the detector, not to the gripper.

The third problem was the quiet one. If a grip is weak enough, the holding current sits
so low that the drop threshold falls below what the servo can even report, and the
detector would simply never fire while looking perfectly healthy from the outside. That
is the worst kind of bug because it produces a plausible looking timeout instead of an
error. So I added a guard that checks whether the computed threshold is degenerate and
reports it as such, rather than letting the run quietly record a non-detection.

The last realisation of the week was about the detection time column itself. I start
the watcher, then I reach over and pull the object. So the number I am recording is the
gap between arming the watch and the current collapsing, which contains my own reaction
time and however long the pull took, and only at the very end the actual detector
latency. That is not the same thing as latency from slip onset, which is what the
papers quote in the hundred millisecond range. I am recording it honestly as what it is
and I will have to be careful never to put my numbers next to theirs as if they
measured the same thing. Fixing it properly would mean sampling position alongside
current in the watch loop and defining onset as the first position deviation past the
settle tolerance, which is a bigger change than I want to make in the middle of a
collection run.

So the week ended with the rig running and the first real rows in the CSV, which after
all those weeks of CAD and code is a genuinely good feeling. The detector is behaving,
the procedure is repeatable, and I finally have numbers instead of intentions. Now I
just have to keep grinding through the matrix.
